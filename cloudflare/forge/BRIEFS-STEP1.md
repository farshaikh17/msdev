# Ten briefs: Step 1

Each business got two or three ideas, written separately from different angles. A judge picked the one that "could only be this business" and threw out any that invented facts. **Read the chosen idea for each and ask: is this about them, and are any two the same?**

Businesses 1–5 are your existing sites, built from only a name, a trade and a town. Businesses 6–10 are five made-up plumbers in Opelousas with one line of "what the rep saw" each, the way the pilot will really look.

## 1. Pointe Coupee Museum (Museum, Parlange, LA)

*What we knew:* name, trade and town only

**The idea:** The visitor should feel that history here is a matter of the river moving on and leaving things behind, so the site is built as a bend in the river: the page curves like an oxbow and each stop is something the water left on its banks.

**Headline:** "The river moved. We kept what it left."

**The true thing it stands on:** This is the museum of Pointe Coupee Parish, kept in the place where the parish's own story happened: the False River country, where a river was cut off and left behind as a lake.  
*Evidence:* The name "Pointe Coupee Museum" and the town "Parlange, LA". Local knowledge: Pointe Coupee means "cut point" in French. False River is an oxbow lake, once the Mississippi's main channel, cut off by the river's change of course. Parlange is the name of the plantation house on its shore, and the parish is a French-Creole, Louisiana-style place with live oaks and sugarcane fields.

**It refuses:** It will not look like a generic plantation-house brochure with white columns, gold frames and hushed reverence, and it will not use stock "history" parchment clichés.

**The home page, screen by screen:**
1. A map-like view of the old river bend: the cut point, and False River left as a lake
2. "Pointe Coupee": the French name explained in one line, a place named for a cut
3. What the water left: a few objects and stories from the parish, each set on a curve of shoreline
4. The people who lived on these banks, in French, Creole and many other voices, told plainly
5. Plan a visit: Parlange, with the address and hours left for the museum to fill in

**Colour:** Muddy Mississippi brown-tan, False River green-grey, live-oak deep green, and a faded cypress cream for the page. A small accent of sugarcane-field gold. All are colours of the water, the silt and the trees of the parish.  
**Lettering:** A sturdy, slightly old-fashioned serif for headlines, with an italic that nods to the French in the place names, and a clean, readable sans for details. It feels like a well-kept regional archive, not a mausoleum.  
**Closest look:** editorial - Magazine-style storytelling with a big serif and quiet earthy colour suits a museum that tells a place's story in chapters.  
**First screen:** own: A full-screen, softly toned map-like drawing shows the Mississippi's old bend, with the cut-off curve of False River glowing as a lake. As the visitor moves the pointer or scrolls, a thin line traces the river's old path to the new one, and the headline settles inside the curve. The museum's name sits small beneath, like a map label. - It shows the place's defining fact, the cut, before a word of history is read.  
**Flying object:** none  
**Big moment:** While scrolling past the "cut point" section, the river line breaks across the page and one bend drifts away into a still lake, so the visitor sees how the name came to be.

*Why the judge chose it:* B builds the whole site from the parish's own name, "cut point," and the oxbow of False River, so it could only belong to this museum; A's accession-drawer idea would fit almost any small museum.

*The other idea(s) it beat:*
- "Keep what the river left behind.": The site works like a museum's accession drawer: each thing from the parish is laid out on a cream card with a typed catalogue label, so the visitor feels the quiet care of keeping and sees how one small place's past get…

## 2. Flower House (Florist, Eunice, LA)

*What we knew:* name, trade and town only

**The idea:** Build the site as a house you walk through, room by room (front porch, kitchen table, church pew, front door), so a visitor understands that flowers belong in every room of a life in Eunice, and that the shop is where they start.

**Headline:** "Every room deserves flowers."

**The true thing it stands on:** A flower shop that calls itself a house: a place where flowers live, and where people in Eunice go to bring something home or send it to someone else's door.  
*Evidence:* "Flower House": the word "House" next to "Flower", and the work being "Florist". Locally, Eunice is a small Cajun prairie town in Acadia Parish, where people know each other and flowers go to church, funerals, dances and kitchen tables.

**It refuses:** It will not look like a generic floral catalogue of glossy bouquets on white, or use cold corporate e-commerce grids.

**The home page, screen by screen:**
1. Front porch: the name on the door, a bloom in the window, "Come on in."
2. Kitchen table: everyday flowers for no reason at all
3. The good room: weddings, anniversaries, and dance-hall nights
4. The pew and the parlor: sympathy flowers, handled gently and with care
5. Out the front door: sending flowers to someone in Eunice and nearby
6. Back on the porch: where to find the shop, how to call, and how to order

**Colour:** Warm porch-ceiling blue-green, cream plaster white, and clay brick red, with a petal pink and marigold yellow accent. These come from a painted Southern house and the flowers around it, and from Louisiana prairie light rather than from any brand.  
**Lettering:** A friendly, slightly old-fashioned serif for headlines, like a hand-painted house sign, with a clean rounded sans for details. It should feel welcoming and domestic, not fashionable.  
**Closest look:** organic: Soft arches like doorways and windows, warm earthy colour and a human touch suit a house made of rooms.  
**First screen:** own: A softly arched front door fills the screen, painted in porch blue-green, with the words "Flower House" over the lintel and flowers spilling around the frame. As the visitor scrolls or taps the door, it swings open to reveal the first room, the kitchen table, with a bouquet in a jar. **Flying object:** none  
**Big moment:** The door opening at the start, from the porch into the first room, then each later section arriving as a new room with its own light and flowers, ending by stepping back out the front door to send flowers.

*Why the judge chose it:* B grows its whole structure out of the one distinctive word in the name, "House," turning the site into rooms of a Eunice life, while A rests on the stock cliché "Say it with flowers" and could belong to any small-town florist.

*The other idea(s) it beat:*

*Evidence:* "Tree Service" in "Eunice, LA". "T Boys" echoes the Cajun French "Ti" naming habit (petit garçon), used for sons and nicknames all over Acadiana.

**It refuses:** It will not use stock-photo forestry (pine-forest panoramas, generic green leaf logos) or corporate "arborist solutions" language.

**The home page, screen by screen:**
1. A live oak over a Eunice yard, its limbs spread low across the lawn: "Big oaks, bad weather. Call the T Boys."
2. The street: a row of yards, each with its tree, tapped to reveal the job (trim, take down, clear after a storm
3. After the storm: a fallen limb across a driveway, and the plain line that says someone will come
4. The prairie trees by name: live oak, pecan, cypress, and what each one needs
5. The ask: a big phone number and a short form, in the voice of a neighbor

**Colour:** Live-oak green, deep and slightly grey; Spanish-moss silver; red-clay brown for earth and fresh-cut wood; a warm cream like porch paint. All of them are colors seen on a Louisiana street, kept earthy so the green does not feel like a corporate brand.  
**Lettering:** A sturdy, slightly rounded slab serif for headlines, like hand-painted truck-door lettering, with a plain, readable sans for details. It has weight like timber, and the plain sans keeps the information clear.  
**Closest look:** americana: Sign-painter craft and local trust fit a small-town trade that works on neighbors' yards.  
**First screen:** own: A full-bleed photo of a big live oak over a quiet Eunice yard, with the headline set in painted-style slab letters across the trunk. Scrolling lowers the camera down the trunk to the street of yards below. – It shows the town's defining tree first, and so it says the work is about the trees people live under.  
**Flying object:** none  
**Big moment:** Storm-clearing screen: the page sky darkens and wind moves the branches, then it clears and a single line appears, "Then we come." It is the one beat that explains why you call.

*Why the judge chose it:* A builds the whole site around a real moment specific to Eunice: standing on the porch looking up at a live oak or pecan. It does this without claiming anything the facts don't support, and the owner could proudly show it off.

*The other idea(s) it beat:*
- "Look up. That tree is your call.": The site is about the tree in the yard and the moment it becomes a worry, so a visitor sees the tree first, large and honestly described, and understands that T Boys is who you call from the porch, looking up at it.
